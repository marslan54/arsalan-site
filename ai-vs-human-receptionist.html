Where the numbers come from. Receptionist pay is the US Bureau of Labor Statistics 2025 median, $38,010 a year or $18.27 an hour (BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook). The uplift is BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation, June 2026: wages are 70.0% of what a private employer pays and benefits the other 30.0%, so the true cost is the wage divided by 0.70 (BLS ECEC). Agent costs use Vapi's published component pricing, read on 21 November 2026 (vapi.ai/pricing), the same model as the running cost calculator.

This is not a like-for-like comparison, and you should not treat it as one. A receptionist greets people at the door, takes deliveries, chases suppliers, calms an angry customer and does a dozen things nobody ever wrote down. An agent answers the phone. If the person you are comparing against does more than answer the phone, the honest version of this sum is how much of their week the phone actually takes.

What is not included. Recruitment, training, cover for holiday and sickness, a desk and a computer on the human side; the one-off build and the optional care plan on the agent side. Both make their column worse, and they roughly do not cancel out.

The honest answer

When you should hire a person instead.

When the caller needs judgment

Complaints, negotiations, bad news, anyone upset. An agent that books appointments is good at booking appointments. It should not be the first thing a distressed customer meets, and a good build routes those calls to a person within seconds.

When somebody has to be there

A front desk with visitors, deliveries and keys needs a human in the chair. The phone is then a fraction of the job, and replacing the phone part saves you a fraction of a salary, not a salary.

When the volume is too low

Under roughly 50 calls a month, per-minute billing is a rounding error and so is the saving. A voicemail that texts the caller back costs almost nothing and may be all you need. I would rather tell you that now than build you something.

The answer is usually "both"

The strongest setup is rarely replacing anyone. It is a person in hours and an agent on the 128 hours a week nobody is paid to be there, plus the overflow when two calls arrive at once. That is the version I build most often.

Questions

About these numbers.

How much does a receptionist really cost an employer?

BLS puts the 2025 median at $38,010 a year, or $18.27 an hour. That is the wage, not the cost. BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation for June 2026 shows wages making up 70.0% of what a private employer pays, with benefits the other 30.0%, so a median receptionist really costs about $26.10 an hour, or roughly $4,500 a month for full-time cover.

Is an AI receptionist cheaper?

On cost per answered call, almost always, because the agent is billed per minute and a salary is paid whether the phone rings or not. But cheaper is the wrong question if the person does more than answer calls. The right question is which hours and which tasks you are actually buying.

How many hours does a full-time person actually cover?

Forty of the 168 hours in a week, which is 24%. The phone rings in the other 128, and that is before holiday, sickness and lunch. That gap is usually a stronger argument than the hourly rate.

Does the agent replace my receptionist?

Not in any build I have done. It takes the calls nobody is there for and the overflow when two arrive at once. The people who are happiest with these systems kept their receptionist and stopped losing the out-of-hours work.

What about an answering service instead?

A reasonable middle option, usually priced per minute or per call with a monthly minimum. They handle nuance better than an agent and cost more per minute; they also generally take a message rather than book into your calendar, which is the thing that actually converts. Worth pricing if your volume is low.

The sum is easy. Whether it pays back in your business is the harder question.
