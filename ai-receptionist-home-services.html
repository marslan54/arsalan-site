An AI receptionist for a home service business, and the one thing that decides whether it works

It is not whether it can hold a conversation. It is what it is allowed to say when a caller asks what something costs.

Guide · 4 min read · 19 November 2026

If you run a plumbing, heating, electrical or similar business, the phone is the business. It also rings while you are under a sink, up a ladder, or driving between jobs. An AI receptionist answers the ones you cannot, and the useful question is not whether it can hold a conversation. It is what it is allowed to say, and what happens when it reaches the edge of what it knows.

What it actually does on a call

A working setup is five steps, and each one is a place where a cheap build goes wrong.

  • It picks up. Your existing number forwards to it, or it answers the overflow when your line is busy. Out of hours it is the only thing standing between a caller and your competitor.
  • It finds out what is wrong. Not a menu. It asks what a good receptionist asks: what has happened, where you are, whether there is water or heat right now, whether this is an emergency.
  • It quotes from a list you approved. This is the part that matters most, and it has its own section below.
  • It books while the caller is still talking. It checks a real calendar, holds a real slot, and says the time out loud. If it cannot hold the slot it must not claim it has.
  • It confirms and hands over. The caller gets a text. You get the transcript. Anything it should not be handling rings a person instead.

The price question, which is where most of them fail

A language model asked “how much to replace a boiler?” will answer. That is what it is built to do. Left to itself it will produce a number that sounds plausible and is not yours, and your customer will hold you to it.

The fix is not a better prompt. It is to take the decision away from the model: prices come from a list you have approved, stored outside the model, and the agent can only read from it. Ask about something that is not on the list and it says it will have someone call you back. That sentence, delivered calmly, is worth more than a clever answer.

If you are being sold a receptionist, ask how prices are stored. If the answer is “it is in the prompt”, the agent can be talked out of them.

What you need before it can go live

  • Your services, in the words your customers use, not your trade's words.
  • A price list you are willing to stand behind, including what is a call-out and what is not.
  • Your hours, and what counts as an emergency outside them.
  • A calendar it can write to, with the slot lengths each job type really takes.
  • One person's number for the calls it should not handle.

That list is usually a morning's work, and it is the difference between a two-week build and a three-month one.

What it costs to run

Two bills, both yours directly and neither marked up by whoever builds it: the telephony minutes and number, and the model usage per call. At low volume this is usually tens of dollars a month, not hundreds. Ask for the per-call figure rather than a monthly estimate, because that is the number that scales with your success.

How to tell a good one from a demo

Demos are easy. Ask for the awkward calls instead:

  • A caller who pushes hard on price, twice.
  • A caller who interrupts halfway through a sentence.
  • A caller who changes their mind about the date.
  • A caller asking about something you do not offer.
  • Two callers wanting the same slot within a minute of each other.

The last one catches most builds. For the voice agent I built, the receptionist runs through 29 offline tests before it answers a real call, and the double-booking case is one of them.

When you should not get one

If you take under a handful of calls a week, a person is cheaper and better. If every job needs a site visit before anything can be quoted, the agent can only book the visit, which may still be worth it but is a smaller prize. And if your calendar lives in your head, fix that first: an agent cannot book into a diary that does not exist.

Questions people ask

Will callers know it is not a person?

Some will and some will not, and you should not try to hide it. The agent gives its name at the start. In practice callers care far more about being answered and getting a time than about who answered.

What happens in an emergency?

It is routed. Emergencies ring the on-call number instead of going into the booking flow, and that rule is set by you, not decided by the model on the day.

Can it work with my existing number?

Yes. The usual setup forwards your current number, either always or only when the line is busy or unanswered, so nothing changes for the people who already have your number.

How long does it take to build?

About two weeks once the price list, hours and calendar are agreed. Most of the delay in these projects is waiting for those, not the build.

What if it gets something wrong?

Every call is transcribed and recorded, so you can see exactly what was said. Fixes in the first two weeks after go-live are included in the way I price this.
